# Arabic Editorial Cover Director

## 1. Mission

Create a front cover that feels like the supplied weekly-journal references **without copying a specific cover literally**. The result must read as a real Arabic editorial magazine/newspaper cover, not as a generic poster.

The cover must communicate, in this order:

1. **One instantly readable political/social metaphor**.
2. **One dominant lead headline** with a controlled red emphasis.
3. **A strong, stable masthead identity** at the top.
4. **Supporting teasers and issue metadata** that create editorial density without competing with the lead.

The visual system is intentionally confrontational, satirical, intellectual, and newspaper-like. It mixes high editorial seriousness with caricature and symbolic visual jokes.

---

## 2. What was observed from the supplied references

These rules are derived from the seven supplied cover images.

### 2.1 Canvas and proportion

- Supplied rasters are approximately **553–558 px wide x 744–748 px high**.
- Observed width/height ratio is approximately **0.745**.
- Preserve this tall magazine ratio unless another output format is explicitly required.
- Recommended working sizes:
  - 1490 x 2000 px
  - 1788 x 2400 px
  - 2235 x 3000 px
- If printing, scale proportionally and add proper bleed separately.

### 2.2 Dominant palette

The recurring system is:

- **Near-black:** `#0B0B0B` to `#171717`
- **Paper white / warm off-white:** `#F2F1EC` to `#E9E7E1`
- **Signature editorial red:** approximately **`#ED1846`**
- Optional sepia paper for cinematic/political-history covers:
  - `#D6C09C`
  - `#C9AC84`
  - deep brown-black shadows

Measured from the supplied raster references, the red accent typically occupies only about **2–3% of the cover area**; one more expressive cover rises to roughly **6%**. Red is therefore an **accent**, not a background color.

### 2.3 Recurring page anatomy

Nearly every cover contains:

- top teaser strip(s),
- oversized Arabic masthead,
- tiny issue/series line under or near the masthead,
- a red paint/hand/stamp-like micro-brand element,
- a dominant hero image occupying most of the central page,
- a large lead headline in black/white/red,
- a short lead deck,
- a narrow vertical metadata rail on the right edge,
- a QR code at lower right,
- one or two bottom teasers,
- occasional upper-left red bracket/callout box.

The system should feel **dense around the edges and spacious around the hero**.

## 3. Core visual DNA

### 3.1 The hero idea must be metaphor-first

Never begin with decoration. Begin with a political/social concept that can be understood visually.

Strong reference archetypes include:

- split or mirrored portrait showing duality/decline,
- politician holding an absurd everyday object,
- human figure transformed into a symbolic object,
- politician represented as a playing card / joker / game piece,
- animal-headed official in formal clothing,
- two opposing institutional figures facing different directions,
- ensemble cast restaged as a film genre or historical tableau.

The visual joke should be legible even before reading the deck.

### 3.2 One cover = one main metaphor

Do not stack several independent metaphors into the same hero image.

Good:
- one person + one transformed object,
- one split portrait,
- one playing-card metaphor,
- one institutional confrontation.

Bad:
- politician + burning flag + globe + chains + explosion + money + parliament + crowd in one image.

### 3.3 Contrast is structural

Use one of these contrast systems:

- **black ink on warm white + red accent**, or
- **sepia cinematic montage + black/white/red typography**, or
- **dark photo background + white masthead + red accent**, or
- **black/white split portrait + one side in color**.

The cover should never become full-spectrum colorful.

## 4. Normalized layout grid

Use percentages of page width/height. Coordinates are approximate design targets, not rigid pixels.

### 4.1 Safe geometry

- Outer visual margin: **3.0–4.5%**
- Keep essential text inside **4%** from trim.
- Allow hero artwork to bleed to the edges when composition benefits from it.
- Do not allow the QR code or issue metadata to bleed.

### 4.2 Top editorial rail

Typical zone:

- `y = 2%–9%`
- full width except margins

Structure:

- 2–3 short teaser blocks
- RTL reading logic
- category label in black
- one red keyword/phrase
- separators may use a slash, thin rule, or spacing

Do not create a colored banner across the page.

### 4.3 Masthead lockup

Typical zone on light covers:

- `x = 45%–94%`
- `y = 9%–22%`

Dark/photo covers can move the masthead more centrally if needed for contrast.

Rules:

- masthead is **very large, black or white**,
- mostly horizontal,
- visually heavier than all secondary teasers,
- red underline / paint stroke may sit beneath,
- tiny issue line may sit above/below,
- small calligraphic/brand detail can appear beside it,
- never place a busy texture directly behind the letterforms unless contrast is extreme.

### 4.4 Upper-left analysis/callout box

Common optional zone:

- `x = 4%–36%`
- `y = 4%–20%`

Treatment:

- 1 short kicker/title line,
- 3–7 lines of small body copy,
- red corner bracket or partial red outline,
- open box preferred over a fully filled panel.

### 4.5 Hero field

General zone:

- `y = 20%–89%`
- may extend from edge to edge

The hero typically occupies **45–70% of total visual mass**.

### 4.6 Lead headline zone

Choose one of three positions based on hero composition:

**A. Lower-center overlay**
- `y = 63%–82%`
- ideal for full-bleed portraits/ensembles

**B. Right-side editorial column**
- `x = 58%–94%`
- `y = 28%–55%`
- ideal when illustration occupies left side

**C. Left-side editorial column**
- `x = 4%–45%`
- `y = 28%–58%`
- ideal when hero sits on right

The headline must not float randomly; it is anchored to a deliberate empty field in the hero composition.

### 4.7 Lead deck

Typical:

- 35–90 Arabic words depending on cover density
- 4–9 short lines
- smaller than headline by a large factor
- aligned with the headline block or placed immediately beneath it

### 4.8 Right vertical metadata rail

Typical:

- `x = 95%–99%`
- `y = 27%–90%`

Contents can include:

- issue/date/price/website/series markers,
- tiny vertical rules,
- a small red issue icon/stamp.

Keep this rail narrow and quiet.

### 4.9 QR code

Typical:

- lower-right corner
- width about **5–7% of page width**
- preserve a clean quiet zone
- black on white only
- never stylize so heavily that it stops scanning

### 4.10 Bottom teaser rail

Typical:

- `y = 92%–99%`

Use 1–2 compact teasers:

- section label,
- short headline,
- one red keyword,
- no large boxes.

## 5. Typography system

### 5.1 General typography character

Use modern Arabic editorial type with these qualities:

- heavy display weight,
- compact letterforms,
- strong black mass,
- high legibility at a distance,
- minimal ornamental flourishes,
- suited to political headlines.

Preferred families are Arabic grotesk / Kufi / neo-Kufi for display, with a readable Arabic sans for smaller copy.

Avoid:

- delicate Naskh as the main headline,
- ornamental Diwani/Thuluth headline styles,
- futuristic tech fonts,
- rounded casual UI fonts.

### 5.2 Hierarchy

Relative size system:

- Masthead: **100% reference size**
- Lead headline: **55–80% of masthead size**
- Major teaser: **16–24%**
- Lead deck: **9–13%**
- Metadata: **5–8%**

The masthead and lead headline should be the only truly massive text elements.

### 5.3 Lead headline construction

Preferred headline length:

- 2–7 words
- 1–3 lines

Use semantic line breaks, not automatic wrapping.

Patterns that fit the system:

- `[concept] [consequence]`
- `من هو [charged noun]؟!`
- `[institution] الذي [verb] [symbol]`
- `مستقبل [country/topic] بين [force A] و[force B]`
- `[metaphor noun] [of consequence]`

### 5.4 Red emphasis in headlines

Use red to highlight:

- one emotionally charged noun,
- one decisive verb,
- one ideological label,
- or one short phrase.

Do **not** turn the whole headline red.

Typical red proportion inside the headline: **20–45% of characters**, depending on line structure.

### 5.5 White headline mode

On dark photography:

- white becomes the base headline color,
- red remains the accent,
- black can be used only if backed by a light shape or stroke.

### 5.6 RTL rules

All Arabic must use proper RTL shaping.

Mandatory:

- use real Arabic text layout,
- preserve contextual glyph shaping,
- punctuation must remain visually correct,
- do not manually reverse strings,
- keep Western digits when they match the publication system,
- verify mixed Arabic + Latin names visually,
- manually control headline line breaks.

Do not rely on an image generator to render Arabic lettering correctly.

## 6. Image and illustration modes

Choose exactly one primary mode per cover.

### MODE 1 — Black ink editorial caricature

Best for:
- domestic politics,
- satire,
- corruption,
- institutional criticism,
- cultural commentary.

Visual treatment:

- off-white or light gray paper,
- thick black contour lines,
- dense cross-hatching,
- etched/engraved shading,
- newspaper cartoon energy,
- selected red object or detail,
- large white negative-space zones reserved for type.

The reference covers use this mode repeatedly.

### MODE 2 — Symbolic black/red illustration

Best for:
- ideology,
- betrayal,
- decay,
- identity politics,
- social critique.

Treatment:

- human form partly transformed into symbol,
- black and grayscale drawing,
- one saturated red object,
- minimal background.

Examples of symbolic object logic:

- flower/head,
- animal/head,
- broken object,
- card/game piece,
- mask,
- mirror/split face.

### MODE 3 — Political playing-card / object metaphor

Best for:
- kingmaker stories,
- hidden power,
- elections,
- elite manipulation.

Treatment:

- oversized physical object tilted in perspective,
- portrait integrated into the object,
- duplicate/upside-down symmetry if conceptually useful,
- warm paper backdrop,
- black/red typography adjacent to object.

### MODE 4 — Split portrait / dual-state photo collage

Best for:
- geopolitical crisis,
- rise vs decline,
- before/after,
- power fracture,
- leadership succession.

Treatment:

- one face split down the middle,
- one side color, one side grayscale,
- hard torn-paper or rough vertical division,
- strong skin/texture detail,
- dark background,
- white masthead/headline where necessary.

### MODE 5 — Opposing figures / institutional confrontation

Best for:
- army vs intelligence,
- palace vs party,
- civil vs military,
- two competing power centers.

Treatment:

- two figures back-to-back or looking away,
- clean white/off-white background,
- black ink or posterized portrait treatment,
- headline bridges the two figures.

### MODE 6 — Cinematic ensemble / genre pastiche

Best for:
- elections,
- many competing actors,
- factional politics,
- “cast of characters” stories.

Treatment:

- 3–5 figures,
- clear central hierarchy,
- genre styling such as western/noir/period poster,
- warm sepia paper or cinematic grading,
- subtle texture/grain,
- large bottom-center headline over the ensemble.

Never make the ensemble look like a random group photo; each person needs a role in the visual narrative.

### MODE 7 — Real-Face Satirical Political Photomontage

Best for:
- current politics,
- diplomatic contradictions,
- public statements versus documented actions,
- geopolitical hypocrisy,
- institutional power struggles,
- stories where recognizable public figures are central to the reporting.

Core principle:

Use **real recognizable faces of the actual public figures involved in the story** whenever their identity materially strengthens the visual argument.

The cover should feel like an editorial photomontage assembled by a political magazine art director, not like a generic AI illustration.

The satire must come primarily from:
- juxtaposition,
- scale,
- props,
- documents,
- labels,
- objects,
- body positioning,
- environmental contrast,
- or an absurd but instantly understandable editorial situation.

Do not rely only on exaggerated facial expressions.

#### Real-face treatment

Preferred treatment:

- photorealistic or documentary-grade recognizable faces,
- authentic age, hairstyle, facial hair and general appearance,
- strong editorial lighting,
- realistic skin texture,
- controlled cutout / collage edges,
- subtle newspaper grain,
- optional partial grayscale treatment,
- consistent lighting across all figures,
- serious magazine-photography quality.

Faces may be slightly stylized for editorial cohesion, but **identity must remain unmistakable**.

Do not:
- replace a real actor with an invented generic politician,
- heavily deform the face,
- make the person look grotesque merely for comedy,
- mix realistic faces with cartoon bodies unless the metaphor specifically requires it.

#### Sarcasm architecture

The preferred satire is **dry, intelligent, visual and evidence-based**.

Use the pattern:

> documented fact A + documented fact B + visual contradiction = satire

Examples of visual devices:

- politician standing beside an oversized receipt,
- diplomatic rivals connected by a cash register,
- leader holding an object contradicting the public rhetoric,
- two leaders separated by a document showing their practical relationship,
- political actor sitting on a stack labelled with competing interests,
- public slogan visually contradicted by the surrounding scene,
- absurdly bureaucratic ledger exposing the contradiction,
- game board where documented political actors occupy opposing pieces,
- suitcase, invoice, cheque, stamp, calculator or filing cabinet used as political metaphor.

The joke should be understood in approximately 2 seconds.

#### One contradiction only

One cover must expose **one main contradiction**.

Good:
- hostility in rhetoric versus cooperation in practice,
- austerity rhetoric versus visible expenditure,
- anti-foreign rhetoric versus dependence on foreign support,
- public promise versus measurable result,
- security language versus economic dependence.

Bad:
- five separate scandals,
- unrelated accusations,
- random symbols of corruption,
- explosions, money, chains, flags and blood all in the same frame.

#### Public-figure integrity rule

When using real public figures:

1. Their presence must be connected directly to the reported story.
2. Do not invent criminal conduct, secret relationships or factual events.
3. Do not fabricate a literal quotation and present it as something the person actually said.
4. Quote bubbles are allowed only for a verified quotation from the edition's sources.
5. If no verified quote exists, use a paraphrased editorial label, a rhetorical question, a symbolic object, or an unlabeled visual contradiction.
6. Satire may exaggerate the **visual metaphor**, not the underlying factual claim.

#### Documentary prop rule

Whenever possible, turn a real reported fact into a physical editorial prop.

Examples:
- reported amount → giant receipt,
- deficit → cracked balance sheet,
- vote result → score board,
- treaty → oversized contract,
- sanctions → price tag,
- aid → shipping manifest,
- debt → invoice,
- election polling → betting board,
- regulation → red bureaucratic stamp.

The prop must simplify the story rather than add a second story.

## 7. Image-generation prompt rules

When generating hero art with an image model, **never ask the model to render the final Arabic cover text**.

The art prompt should explicitly request:

- **no words, no letters, no logo, no QR code, no typography**,
- the needed blank/quiet zones for masthead and headline,
- portrait orientation matching the cover ratio,
- clear subject separation,
- editorial illustration rather than generic advertising art.

### 7.1 Base prompt skeleton — ink caricature

> Portrait editorial political caricature for an Arabic weekly magazine cover, warm off-white paper, high-contrast black ink linework, cross-hatching and etched newspaper texture, one sharp symbolic metaphor, expressive but recognizable facial anatomy, controlled satirical exaggeration, strong negative space for Arabic headline on [left/right], clean upper region for masthead, one isolated object suitable for later red accent, no text, no letters, no logo, no watermark, no QR code.

### 7.2 Base prompt skeleton — split photo collage

> Portrait editorial cover artwork, dramatic split portrait of the same political figure, one half realistic color and one half monochrome, rough torn vertical seam, dark textured background, very high micro-contrast, documentary-political mood, top area kept readable for a large masthead, lower area kept open for a two-line headline, no text, no logo, no watermark, no QR code.

### 7.3 Base prompt skeleton — cinematic ensemble

> Portrait political editorial cover artwork, 4-character cinematic ensemble staged like a vintage [western/noir/period] poster, strong central hierarchy, warm sepia paper texture, realistic faces with controlled stylization, serious political satire rather than comedy, open top band for masthead and open lower band for headline, no text, no letters, no logo, no watermark, no QR code.

### 7.4 Base prompt skeleton — real-face satirical photomontage

> Portrait editorial political photomontage for a serious Arabic weekly magazine cover. Use recognizable realistic likenesses of the actual public figures involved in the reported story. Documentary photography quality, authentic facial anatomy, realistic skin texture, controlled dramatic lighting, gritty newspaper grain, warm off-white / charcoal / restrained editorial red palette. Build one sharp visual contradiction using [DOCUMENTED FACT / PROP]. The satire should feel dry, intelligent and politically literate rather than cartoonish. Strong composition with clear hierarchy, clean upper region reserved for masthead and lower region reserved for headline. No text, no letters, no invented quotes, no logos, no QR code, no watermark.

## 8. Compositing and image treatment

### 8.1 Do not flatten text into the hero image

Final cover typography should be rendered separately in SVG/HTML/CSS/InDesign/Figma/Photoshop or another layout engine.

Reason:

- Arabic needs correct shaping,
- headline line breaks must be editorially controlled,
- QR and metadata must remain sharp,
- red/black hierarchy must be exact.

### 8.2 Ink illustration processing

If starting from a photo or generated portrait:

1. remove background,
2. convert to high-contrast grayscale,
3. add contour/ink edges,
4. introduce cross-hatching or stippled engraving texture,
5. keep facial planes readable,
6. isolate one visual element for red,
7. preserve paper white in open regions.

Do not apply a generic “sketch” filter to the entire page.

### 8.3 Paper texture

Texture must be visible but secondary.

Recommended:

- very fine grain,
- mild fiber/noise,
- subtle tonal variation,
- no giant stains unless conceptually motivated.

### 8.4 Sepia treatment

For history/election/ensemble covers:

- warm beige base,
- brown-black shadows,
- faded highlights,
- mild vignette,
- red remains clean and contemporary to contrast the vintage image.

## 9. Editorial copy architecture

### 9.1 Lead headline

- 2–7 words preferred.
- Must contain a clear thesis, metaphor, or provocation.
- Avoid vague abstractions.
- Use one red semantic anchor.

### 9.2 Lead deck

- Give context, not repetition.
- Explain why the visual metaphor matters.
- 35–90 Arabic words.
- Keep paragraphs compact.
- Avoid more than one long paragraph on the cover.

### 9.3 Top teasers

Use 2–3.

Each should contain:

- section tag,
- 5–13 word headline,
- one red word/phrase,
- no body paragraph unless it is the upper-left callout feature.

### 9.4 Bottom teasers

Use 1–2.

Keep them shorter than top teasers.

### 9.5 Quote bubble/stamp

Optional small circular or oval stamp can be used for:

- a provocative quote,
- “exclusive” style micro-copy,
- a tiny rhetorical aside.

Use sparingly: one only.

## 10. Signature micro-elements

These details strongly contribute to the reference look.

### 10.1 Red paint/hand/stamp mark

Place near the masthead or issue line.

Characteristics:

- irregular organic outline,
- flat editorial red,
- tiny white/black micro-copy may sit inside if needed,
- small enough not to become a second hero.

### 10.2 Red underline / brush swash

Often placed beneath the masthead.

It should feel hand-painted or torn, not like a clean UI rectangle.

### 10.3 Partial red bracket

For upper-left analysis text:

- use an L-shaped or three-sided thin red rule,
- do not use a full heavy border.

### 10.4 Vertical right-edge type

Very small, gray/black, sometimes rotated 90 degrees.

This gives the page a print-publication feel.

## 11. Cover archetypes derived from the seven references

Use these as layout presets.

### Preset A — Dark split-power cover

- dark full-bleed portrait background,
- split face/color-state,
- white masthead,
- lead headline low and centered,
- red word inside headline,
- body/deck beneath,
- white top teasers.

### Preset B — White caricature + right headline

- white/off-white background,
- large black-ink figure on left/center,
- headline on upper-right,
- one red line/word,
- body copy under headline,
- minimal overlap.

### Preset C — Symbolic figure + left headline

- hero on right,
- large 2–3 line headline on left,
- red symbol integrated into hero,
- quote marks or pull-quote can reinforce the theme.

### Preset D — Object metaphor + right text column

- large tilted object/card on left 55–65%,
- headline/deck as a clean right column,
- warm paper background,
- small teaser rail above.

### Preset E — Animal/absurdist portrait + right text

- large black ink animal-human hybrid on left,
- red quote marks or one red phrase on right,
- strong white field,
- article deck in a narrow readable block.

### Preset F — Dual institutional portrait

- two figures fill mid-page,
- clean white field,
- headline runs horizontally across lower torso area,
- red first line + black second line or vice versa,
- deck sits beneath in small type.

### Preset G — Sepia ensemble election cover

- 3–5 characters across mid/lower page,
- textured sepia paper,
- masthead black on top,
- headline large across lower-middle,
- one strong red noun,
- compact deck and bottom teasers.

### Preset H — Face vs Face + Evidence Object

- two real public figures,
- left/right confrontation,
- one oversized factual object between them,
- hero occupies 55–70% of the cover,
- masthead remains clean above,
- large lower headline,
- one red contradiction word.

Ideal for diplomacy, money, negotiations and geopolitical contradictions.

### Preset I — Single Real Face + Absurd Prop

- one dominant public figure,
- documentary-style portrait,
- one oversized symbolic object,
- clean off-white or dark editorial background,
- short provocative headline.

Ideal for institutional decisions, leadership, economic policy and personal political responsibility.

### Preset J — Real-Face Political Ensemble

- 3–5 actual public figures,
- strong central hierarchy,
- each figure has a clear narrative role,
- realistic faces with cinematic photomontage treatment,
- satire comes from staging rather than facial deformation,
- one shared visual metaphor.

Ideal for elections, coalition politics and multi-actor geopolitical stories.

## 12. Headline color logic

Use one of these patterns:

- **BLACK / RED**
- **RED / BLACK**
- **WHITE / RED** on dark art
- **BLACK / WHITE / RED** over textured photography

Never use more than these three headline colors.

Use red for semantic emphasis, not alternating decoration.

---

## 13. Density control

The references are information-rich but not cluttered.

Target visual density:

- top 20%: dense editorial identity,
- middle 55–60%: hero-dominant,
- lower 20–25%: headline/deck/footer information.

If a side contains a large text block, the opposite side should carry the hero mass.

Do not fill every empty area.

Negative space is a deliberate part of the style.

---

## 14. Face and subject handling

For political figures:

- preserve recognizable facial structure,
- stylize through linework, props, framing, and texture rather than grotesque deformation,
- exaggeration can be satirical but must remain editorially controlled,
- eyes and hands should be anatomically coherent,
- avoid AI-art artifacts in fingers, glasses, insignia, hats, and facial hair.

For multiple figures:

- keep consistent lighting/stylization,
- do not mix photoreal, oil painting, vector, and sketch styles in one hero.

---

## 15. Background rules

Preferred backgrounds:

- warm off-white paper,
- light gray paper,
- beige/sepia paper,
- dark documentary texture for crisis covers.

Avoid:

- gradients with no editorial purpose,
- neon colors,
- abstract 3D blobs,
- stock-office backgrounds,
- glossy commercial-advertising lighting.

---

## 16. What makes the reference style feel authentic

The authenticity comes from the combination of:

- **one conceptual visual punchline**,
- **serious newspaper typography**,
- **highly controlled red**,
- **dense teaser system at the top and bottom**,
- **micro print details on the side**,
- **a huge recognizable masthead**,
- **strong editorial illustration**, not generic social-media design,
- **Arabic RTL typesetting that looks manually art-directed**.

Removing any one of these weakens the look; removing several turns it into a poster instead of a journal cover.

---

## 17. Failure modes to reject

Reject the cover if any of these are true:

- hero image has random text generated inside it,
- Arabic letters are malformed or disconnected,
- masthead is too small,
- lead headline competes with 4–5 equally large teasers,
- red is used as a full-page dominant color,
- hero is generic stock photography,
- no clear metaphor is visible,
- too many visual symbols are combined,
- QR is too small or has no quiet zone,
- page lacks right-side metadata and teaser architecture,
- bottom feels empty like a poster,
- typography is centered everywhere,
- excessive boxes/panels make it look like a website dashboard,
- illustration is overly polished/3D and loses newspaper bite,
- cover uses more than one unrelated illustration style,
- real public figures are used even though they are not materially connected to the story,
- a quote bubble contains an invented or unverified quotation,
- satire depends on a factual claim that is not supported by the edition's reporting,
- a real-face cover becomes grotesque parody instead of controlled editorial satire.

## 18. Production workflow

### Step 1 — Parse editorial input

Extract:

- lead story,
- political/social conflict,
- key actors,
- documented contradiction,
- public figures suitable for recognizable real-face treatment,
- one factual number/document/object that can become the satirical prop,
- verified quotes usable on cover, if any,
- one visual metaphor,
- one primary emotional tone,
- 2–3 supporting stories,
- publication metadata.

### Step 2 — Choose one archetype

Select Preset A–J based on the lead story.

When the lead story contains recognizable public figures and a strong documented contradiction, prefer **MODE 7** with Preset H, I, or J over an abstract illustration, unless another mode communicates the story more clearly.

### Step 3 — Reserve text zones before creating art

Define:

- masthead zone,
- headline zone,
- deck zone,
- top teaser strip,
- right metadata rail,
- QR zone,
- bottom teaser rail.

### Step 4 — Generate or prepare hero art

Generate **art only**. No typography.

### Step 5 — Compose in grayscale first

Check that the cover reads without red.

If hierarchy fails in grayscale, fix layout before color.

### Step 6 — Add signature red

Apply red to:

- 1 headline word/phrase,
- 1 hero detail or micro-brand,
- optional thin rules/stamps.

### Step 7 — Add editorial micro-copy

Top teasers, upper-left callout, right rail, bottom teasers.

### Step 8 — Add QR and metadata

Keep them sharp and secondary.

### Step 9 — RTL and punctuation audit

Manually inspect every Arabic line.

### Step 10 — Thumbnail test

At approximately phone-thumbnail size, the viewer must still see:

1. masthead,
2. hero concept,
3. lead headline,
4. red emphasis.

If not, simplify.

## 19. Quality gate checklist

A cover is COMPLETE only if all answers are YES.

### Concept

- [ ] Is there exactly one dominant visual metaphor?
- [ ] Can the visual idea be understood without reading the body copy?
- [ ] Does the art relate directly to the lead story?

### Hierarchy

- [ ] Is the masthead unmistakable?
- [ ] Is the lead headline the second major typographic mass?
- [ ] Are supporting teasers clearly subordinate?

### Color

- [ ] Is the palette mostly black/off-white or black/sepia?
- [ ] Is editorial red close to `#ED1846`?
- [ ] Is red limited and purposeful?

### Typography

- [ ] Is Arabic correctly shaped RTL?
- [ ] Are headline line breaks manually chosen?
- [ ] Is red used for semantic emphasis?
- [ ] Are small text blocks readable at full size?

### Artwork
